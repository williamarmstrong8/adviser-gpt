Every year we survey asset managers and wealth advisers on how they handle RFPs, DDQs, and consultant questionnaires. This year we heard from more than 400 firms across institutional and wealth channels. Here’s a detailed snapshot of what we’re seeing in 2026—and what it means for teams who want to respond faster without sacrificing quality or compliance.

## Volume and turnaround

RFP and DDQ volume continues to rise. In our 2026 survey, 78% of respondents said they received more questionnaire requests in the past 12 months than in the prior year. Only 6% reported a decrease. The average firm now fields roughly 200 to 400 unique RFPs and DDQs per year, with larger asset managers and multi-strategy shops often exceeding 600.

Turnaround expectations from consultants and allocators have not relaxed. The majority of firms report that standard request windows remain between five and fifteen business days, with “rush” requests sometimes as short as 48 to 72 hours. Teams are under constant pressure to deliver complete, accurate, and compliant responses on time—often with the same or smaller headcount than they had two years ago.

This combination—higher volume, tighter deadlines, and flat or shrinking resources—is the central challenge of RFP response in 2026. Firms that don’t change how they work are either burning out their teams, missing opportunities, or both.

## Where time goes

We asked respondents to break down where their team’s time actually goes during a typical RFP or DDQ cycle. The answers were consistent across firm size and strategy type.

**Finding and reusing prior answers** remains the single largest time sink. On average, teams report spending 35–45% of their response time searching for the right prior response, checking whether it’s still current, and deciding how to adapt it to the new question. In firms without a [single source of truth](/products) (e.g., a centralized content library or [Vault](/products)), that number can exceed 50%. Version control and “which document had the approved language?” are recurring pain points.

**Tailoring and drafting** is the next biggest category. Even when a prior answer exists, someone has to adapt the language to the specific question, update data points, and ensure tone and positioning are consistent. This typically consumes another 25–35% of cycle time. Duplicate or near-duplicate questions across different questionnaires add to the burden, because many firms still answer them from scratch each time.

**Compliance and legal review** accounts for roughly 15–25% of the cycle. Reviewers are checking for accuracy, consistency with approved messaging, and regulatory alignment. Bottlenecks here often stem from last-minute submissions and ad hoc language that doesn’t match pre-approved content.

**Administrative and formatting** tasks—tracking deadlines, assembling documents, managing versions, and formatting final submissions—round out the remainder. Though often underestimated, these activities can add another 10–15% to total cycle time.

Firms that centralize content and use AI-assisted drafting report meaningful reductions in time-to-first-draft, often in the 40–60% range. The gains are largest when the system is grounded in the firm’s own approved language, so that first drafts are already close to what compliance expects.

## Technology adoption and barriers

Adoption of dedicated [RFP and DDQ tools](/solutions/rfp-teams)—including AI-assisted drafting—has continued to climb. In 2026, about 52% of survey respondents said their firm uses some form of technology beyond spreadsheets and shared drives to manage questionnaire content and responses, up from roughly 38% in 2024. Of those, a growing share is using AI to generate or suggest answers, not just to store and retrieve them.

The main barriers to adoption remain what they were a year ago: concern about compliance and control, uncertainty about accuracy, and resistance to changing long-standing workflows. Firms that have adopted AI-assisted drafting successfully tend to emphasize three things: (1) the AI is restricted to the firm’s own approved content, (2) there is a clear review step before anything goes out, and (3) the tool fits into existing workflows (e.g., [Word, Excel](/products)) rather than requiring a separate platform for every task.

Trust and transparency matter. Features like [Trust Score](/products) or equivalent—showing how close a generated answer is to pre-approved language—help compliance and business teams feel confident that the technology is supporting, not replacing, their judgment.

## Compliance and consistency

Compliance and legal teams are under the same volume pressure as the rest of the firm. In our survey, 71% of respondents agreed that “our compliance team is asked to review more content than they can realistically review in depth.” The result is often a triage approach: heavy review on high-stakes or novel language, lighter review on content that clearly repeats approved messaging.

That’s one reason consistency is so important. When answers are pulled from a single, approved source and minimally altered, review is faster and risk is lower. When every response is written from scratch or heavily paraphrased, review time and regulatory risk both go up. Firms that have invested in a [single source of truth](/products) for RFP and DDQ content report fewer compliance findings and faster sign-off, even as volume increases.

Regulatory expectations have not eased. SEC and other regulators continue to focus on accuracy of client-facing and prospect-facing materials, including DDQ and RFP responses. Firms that can demonstrate a controlled, repeatable process—and an audit trail from approved content to final submission—are in a stronger position.

## Benchmarks by firm size

We sliced the data by firm size to see how challenges and practices differ.

**Small teams (roughly 1–10 people involved in RFP/DDQ response)** often have the most acute resource constraints. They report the highest share of time spent on finding and reusing prior answers, in part because content may live in a few people’s heads or in scattered documents. Tools that centralize content and suggest answers from that content tend to have the largest impact here. Small teams also report the highest willingness to try AI-assisted drafting, provided it’s compliant and easy to use.

**Mid-size firms (roughly 10–50 people in the response process)** face a different challenge: coordination. Multiple people may be answering different sections of the same questionnaire, and consistency across sections is a common problem. Centralized content and shared drafting tools help, but process and ownership (who updates what, when) matter as much as technology.

**Large organizations (50+ people involved)** often have dedicated [RFP/DDQ teams](/solutions/rfp-teams) and well-established processes. Even so, they report that volume and complexity are growing. Many [asset managers](/solutions/asset-managers) are looking to AI not to replace their team, but to speed first drafts, keep data and narrative in sync, and free experienced staff for higher-value work (e.g., relationship management, custom questions).

Across all segments, the firms that are most satisfied with their current process are those that have combined clear ownership, a [single source of truth](/products) for content, and technology that fits into daily workflows.

## What allocators and consultants expect

We also spoke with allocators and consultants about what they expect from managers in 2026. Three themes came up repeatedly.

**Speed still matters.** Allocators have many managers to evaluate. Slow or incomplete responses can delay due diligence and, in some cases, cause a manager to be deprioritized or dropped from consideration. Fast, complete responses are table stakes.

**Consistency and accuracy are non-negotiable.** Inconsistencies between one questionnaire and another—or between the DDQ and the actual portfolio or process—raise red flags. So do outdated numbers or descriptions. Allocators notice when a firm’s materials don’t align.

**Clarity and relevance.** Long, generic answers are less helpful than concise, relevant ones. Allocators prefer responses that directly address the question and reflect the firm’s actual approach, not boilerplate that could apply to anyone.

These expectations reinforce the value of a strong content foundation and a process that produces consistent, accurate, and timely responses—whether or not the firm uses AI to get there.

## Looking ahead

We expect volume and time pressure to continue. Regulatory focus on accuracy and consistency in client-facing materials is unlikely to ease. The firms that thrive will be those that treat RFP and DDQ response as a core capability: clear ownership, centralized and approved content, and technology that helps people work faster without sacrificing quality or control.
